# The OEI's actual legal status

This page has the sole purpose of stating, precisely and unambiguously, what the International Order of Information Technology Experts (OEI) is today from a legal standpoint — and what it is not. It is addressed in particular to those who need to verify this: lawyers, journalists, regulators, institutional partners.

## What the OEI is today

The OEI is, at this stage, a **founding movement** resting on an **association** — a French "loi 1901" association, or an association within the meaning of Articles 60 et seq. of the Swiss Civil Code, depending on which jurisdiction is ultimately chosen for the entity carrying the movement. This choice has not yet been finalised; it will be made with the support of a legal professional, as it carries tax and liability consequences that go beyond the scope of this page.

The OEI is **not** a professional order in the legal sense of the term. The distinction deserves a simple explanation, as it is often misunderstood. A professional order — that of doctors, lawyers, chartered accountants — is created by a law that grants it three attributes that no private organisation can claim for itself: a protected title, whose use is reserved to those who meet conditions set by law; reserved acts, meaning tasks that only members of the order are entitled to perform; and enforceable disciplinary power, which can suspend or expel a member. An association, however good its work, legally holds none of these three attributes. It may adopt the word "Order" as a working name — as we do — but the word then designates only a horizon and an ambition, never an acquired legal status.

## The two-tier structure

The "International Order of Information Technology Experts" movement will be carried by a distinct, neutral legal entity — envisaged under a name such as OEI Foundation or International Institute for Computing Accountability — rather than by an entity that would itself carry the word "Order" in its corporate name. This separation is not a device meant to blur the lines; it is the opposite. It aims to avoid any confusion between the movement's working name, which is international and unifying in ambition, and the legal name of the structure that bears its legal responsibility, which must remain unambiguously identifiable as an association and not as a constituted order. This is a deliberate clarification, decided before any outside challenge, not a response to one.

## International ambition and legal reality

The OEI carries an international ambition: to bring together, across borders, shared thinking and practice on the accountability of the computing profession. This ambition must not be confused with a single, global legal existence. The registered office, the legal name of the entity carrying the movement, and its territorial representations will be distinguished — and coordinated — country by country, according to the legal framework specific to each territory. No single jurisdiction has, to date, been settled on as a definitive seat.

## What the OEI cannot do today

In the interest of honesty, the current limits must be stated explicitly. The OEI issues no protected title. It defines no reserved act and does not condition access to any profession. It holds no disciplinary power enforceable against third parties. Any certification it might issue, for as long as it has no legal recognition, has no legal value: it confers no protected title and does not condition the exercise of any activity. Its value, at this stage, is solely that of a quality signal, comparable to those produced by technical organisations recognised for the rigour of their work rather than for any legal power.

## Planned trajectory

What follows will be built through sequential milestones, each conditioning the next, with no calendar date announced: legal and identity lock-in (constitution of the association, securing of the name); building a documentary foundation and then a complete body of work (code of ethics, competency framework, charters); assembling a first circle of credible contributors; and, only then, broader recognition — academic partnerships, participation in public consultations. Should more formal institutional recognition come one day, it will always follow the building of credibility, never precede it.
