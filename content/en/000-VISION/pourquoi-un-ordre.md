# Why an Order?

These questions are put to us regularly, by well-informed interlocutors — economists, lawyers, institutional decision-makers. They are legitimate, and we prefer to answer them directly rather than avoid them.

### Why the word "Order" when it is not a legal order?

Because a professional order is created by law, within the framework of a regulated profession with reserved acts, and no private organisation can claim that status for itself — we say so ourselves, plainly, from the very first page of our White Paper. The Order of Computer Experts is, at this stage, a founding movement resting on an association. The word "Order" is therefore not used as a legal title we would have appropriated, but as a working name and a horizon: it designates what we aim to build — a profession equipped with a shared code of ethics, a competency framework and a requirement for continuing education — and the vocabulary that will one day, should our credibility warrant it, allow a more formal discussion to begin. We prefer this name, openly claimed and explained, to a more neutral label that would mask our real ambition. To avoid any legal ambiguity, the legal entity carrying the movement will be registered under a distinct institutional name (for example, OEI Foundation), with an explicit statement that it carries the "Order of Computer Experts" movement — a two-tier structure that clearly separates the neutral legal name from the movement's name, which carries our vision.

### Doesn't a professional order mainly serve to restrict access to a profession?

This is the most common fear, and we understand it. But it inverts the order of our priorities. Our approach starts from the protection of the public: when a computer system controls an aircraft, a hospital, a bank or an electricity grid, an error can have major human and economic consequences, and society is entitled to expect guarantees from those who bear this responsibility. It is this protection which, if effective, generates trust in critical systems; this trust, in turn, raises the overall quality of professional practice. Only at the end of this path, and only as a consequence, can a broader recognition of the profession follow — never as a starting point. We seek neither monopoly nor artificial barriers: our requirements apply to a narrow scope, that of critical systems, without erecting entrenched positions that would freeze a profession whose openness has been its strength.

### Who can call themselves a "computer expert"?

Neither a degree alone nor experience alone is sufficient to determine this. We conceive of expertise as a verifiable combination of several dimensions: up-to-date knowledge, concrete experience, demonstrable achievements, responsibility genuinely exercised in technical decisions, judgment proven in real situations, effective continuing education, and a commitment to professional ethics. This is why our competency framework envisions a graduated scale — Practitioner, Engineer, Architect, Expert, Senior Expert, Fellow — rather than a binary threshold. This scale is not a hierarchy of prestige but a gradation of responsibility and autonomy, which recognises that neither an old degree without up-to-date practice, nor long experience without rigour or continuing education, is sufficient to guarantee the competence on which the safety of critical systems depends.

### Why "international" when you don't yet have legal status in every country?

Because the problem we document knows no borders. The paradox between rigorously standardised technical objects — protocols, security, critical safety — and a profession that is standardised almost nowhere is found, with local nuances, in nearly every country we have studied: France, Switzerland, Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany. None has given computing a complete professional framework comparable to that of medicine or law. Our ambition, therefore, from the outset, is to open an international discussion rather than replicate an isolated national framework that would solve only a fraction of the problem. This does not prejudge a uniform legal status, which we do not have and do not claim to obtain in one stroke: our strategy is to build first, wherever possible, a credible body of work and academic and professional partnerships, then to contribute, country by country and only where the legal framework allows it, to discussions on more formal recognition.

### Will the existence of the OEI reduce cybercrime?

Not mechanically, and we would be careful not to promise that. An Order, even one fully recognised, does not eliminate software vulnerabilities or malicious actors. What we aim for is more modest and more realistic: by raising the level of verifiable competence, by formalising a shared code of ethics, by requiring continuing education in areas such as cybersecurity, and by holding professionals accountable for their technical choices rather than allowing them to disclaim responsibility, we help improve practices that do have a demonstrated effect on the frequency and severity of incidents. The security of a system never depends solely on the compliance of its components with standards: it depends on the judgment and integrity of the professionals who assemble them. It is on this human factor, largely neglected today, that we believe we can have a real effect — without ever claiming a direct, mechanical causality on global cybercrime statistics.
